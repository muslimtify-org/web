Six parts in, the five prayer times are one formula asked five ways, and a table of what each country adds on top. This last part is about the cases where that is not enough.

Two of them are about the sky: places where the sun never gets 18° below the horizon, and places where it never sets at all. One is about the clock, which is a government decision and not an astronomical one, and gets more people's prayer times wrong than any angle ever did. Then the whole program, in one file, checked against the production library for every day of a year.

## When the sun does not get there

Run the program from part 6 for London on 21 June and the Fajr and Isha cells come back empty. The formula is not broken. At latitude 51.5° in midsummer the sun only sinks to about 15° below the horizon at midnight. It never reaches 18°, so there is no moment that satisfies the Muslim World League's definition of Fajr, and `hour_angle` says so by returning nothing.

How often this happens depends on how far north you are. The picture to hold in mind is the sun's altitude at solar midnight, the lowest point of its daily circle. It is simply your latitude plus the declination, minus 90°.

For Jakarta the question never arises. For London the sun stays above 18° down from 23 May to 21 July, sixty days. For Oslo it is April to August. For Tromsø, inside the Arctic Circle, the sun does not set at all for two months, and then in winter does not rise for two months, so there is no sunrise, no Maghrib, and no shadow for Asr either.

### What the texts say

This is not a modern problem invented by astronomers. The Companions asked the Prophet ﷺ about a day that would last a year, in the hadith of the Dajjal, and he answered:

<p dir="rtl" lang="ar" style={{fontSize: '1.45rem', lineHeight: 2.1}}>لَا، اقْدُرُوا لَهُ قَدْرَهُ</p>

> "No. Estimate for it its measure." (Sahih Muslim 2937) {/* VERIFY: number and wording, narrated by an-Nawwas ibn Sam'an */}

That one word, *uqduru*, estimate, is the textual basis every authority leans on for the far north. When the sign in the sky does not come, you do not abandon the prayer, you estimate its time by a measure. The disagreement is about which measure.

### The three measures

Three rules are in common use, and all three take the length of the real night, from sunset to sunrise, and place Fajr and Isha some fraction of the way into it.

The **middle of the night** rule puts Fajr and Isha both at the midpoint, so there is always a night that ends and a night that begins. The **one seventh** rule puts Isha one seventh of the night after sunset and Fajr one seventh before sunrise. The **angle-based** rule, which praytimes.org popularized and most libraries now use, takes the method's own angle as the fraction: for an 18° method, Fajr is 18/60 of the night before sunrise. The idea is that on a normal night the sun covers roughly 60° of its circle between sunset and sunrise, so 18° of twilight is 18/60 of the night, and the rule carries that proportion into nights where the real angle is out of reach.

The angle-based rule has a practical virtue: on the last day before the gap and the first day after, the real time and the substitute are nearly the same, so the timetable does not jump. libmuslim uses it for every method whenever a real night exists, because when its authors checked the Muslim World League's own published London tables for June and July 2026, the angle-based rule reproduced them to within a minute and the alternatives did not. Whoever computes those tables uses this rule, so this is what "the MWL method" means in practice at high latitudes.

### Inside the polar circle

When there is no sunset there is no night to take a fraction of, and all three rules fall silent. Some apps then borrow a day from a lower latitude: compute the whole timetable as if the city sat at 45° (or, for the Moonsighting Committee, 60°) on the same meridian, and use that. libmuslim does this for the polar case, which is why it prints a full Tromsø timetable in June. The code in this series does not. For Tromsø on 21 June it prints Dhuhr and Asr, which exist, and leaves the rest blank.

Both answers are defensible, and that is the point. Borrowing a latitude is a fatwa, not a calculation, and the scholars who have addressed it do not agree. A timetable that prints a borrowed time without saying so is claiming more than it knows. If you ever ship this code to users above 66°, mark the substituted times as substituted. The hadith says estimate, it does not say pretend.

## Clocks are political too

Everything in this series has taken a `timezone` number: 7 for Jakarta, 1 for London in June. That number is not astronomy. It is whatever the government says the clock reads, and governments change their minds.

Egypt is the example to remember. Cairo is UTC+2 in winter and, since daylight saving time was reinstated in 2023, UTC+3 from the last Friday of April to the last Thursday of October. In 2026 that is 24 April to 29 October. {/* VERIFY: the current Egyptian DST decision */} Run the program for Cairo on 17 October with the Egyptian method:

```text
Cairo, 17 October 2026, egypt    fajr   dhuhr  asr    maghrib isha
with UTC+3 (summer time, correct) 05:32  12:41  15:56  18:23  19:40
with UTC+2 (winter time, wrong)   04:32  11:41  14:56  17:23  18:40
```

The sun did the same thing in both rows. Pass the wrong offset and every prayer is an hour early, and a reader fasting in Ramadan would break their fast with the sun still up. There is no astronomical check that can catch this, because the astronomy is identical. The only defence is to get the offset from a source that knows the rules: the operating system's timezone database, which tracks every country's DST decisions and is updated several times a year. libmuslim ships a `timezone.h` for exactly this reason, and Muslimtify asks the system rather than the user.

Indonesia makes it easy, with three fixed zones and no summer time. Britain, most of Europe, the United States and Egypt make it hard. The London figure above is drawn on the civil clock, and the two one-hour steps in it, in March and October, are not the sun moving. They are the clocks.

One more clock problem hides in the Oslo row of the table below. Isha comes out at 00:12. That is twelve minutes past midnight on 22 June, but it is the Isha of 21 June, and a timetable that prints it in the 22 June row has moved it by a day. Times in this series are hours from the midnight that starts the date you asked for, so a value above 24 means "after midnight, still tonight". Keep it that way until the moment you print.

This replaces `prayer_times` from part 6. The only change is the substitute: when Fajr or Isha comes back empty and a real night exists, place it at the method's angle as a share of the night. When there is no night, leave it empty.

def prayer_times(year, month, day, latitude, longitude, timezone, method, shadow_factor=1):
    """The five prayer times as local clock hours, with the method's offsets, precaution
    and the high-latitude substitute applied. None means the time does not exist."""
    jd = julian_day(year, month, day)
    sunrise = sun_event(jd, latitude, longitude, timezone, SUNRISE_ALTITUDE, -1)
    sunset = sun_event(jd, latitude, longitude, timezone, SUNRISE_ALTITUDE, +1)
    night = None if sunrise is None or sunset is None else (24 - sunset) + sunrise  # hours of darkness
    maghrib = None if sunset is None else sunset + method["maghrib"] / 60

    fajr = sun_event(jd, latitude, longitude, timezone, -method["fajr"], -1)
    if fajr is None and night is not None:
        fajr = sunrise - method["fajr"] / 60 * night  # angle-based substitute: the angle as a share of the night
    if method["interval"]:
        isha = None if maghrib is None else maghrib + method["interval"] / 60
    else:
        isha = sun_event(jd, latitude, longitude, timezone, -method["isha"], +1)
        if isha is None and night is not None:
            isha = maghrib + method["isha"] / 60 * night

    times = {
        "fajr": fajr,
        "dhuhr": solar_noon(jd, longitude, timezone),
        "asr": asr(jd, latitude, longitude, timezone, shadow_factor),
        "maghrib": maghrib,
        "isha": isha,
    }
    return {name: None if t is None else t + method["ihtiyat"] / 60 for name, t in times.items()}

/** The five prayer times as local clock hours, with the method's offsets, precaution and the
 *  high-latitude substitute applied. null means the time does not exist. */
function prayerTimes(year: number, month: number, day: number, lat: number, lon: number, tz: number, m: Method, shadowFactor = 1): Times {
  const jd = julianDay(year, month, day);
  const sunrise = sunEvent(jd, lat, lon, tz, SUNRISE_ALTITUDE, -1);
  const sunset = sunEvent(jd, lat, lon, tz, SUNRISE_ALTITUDE, 1);
  const night = sunrise === null || sunset === null ? null : 24 - sunset + sunrise; // hours of darkness
  const maghrib = sunset === null ? null : sunset + m.maghrib / 60;

  let fajr = sunEvent(jd, lat, lon, tz, -m.fajr, -1);
  if (fajr === null && night !== null) fajr = sunrise! - (m.fajr / 60) * night; // angle-based substitute: the angle as a share of the night
  let isha: number | null;
  if (m.interval) {
    isha = maghrib === null ? null : maghrib + m.interval / 60;
  } else {
    isha = sunEvent(jd, lat, lon, tz, -m.isha, 1);

import math

def julian_day(year, month, day):
    if month <= 2:
        year -= 1
        month += 12
    a = year // 100
    b = 2 - a + a // 4
    return math.floor(365.25 * (year + 4716)) + math.floor(30.6001 * (month + 1)) + day + b - 1524.5

def sun_position(jd):
    d = jd - 2451545.0  # days since J2000.0
    g = math.radians((357.529 + 0.98560028 * d) % 360)
    q = (280.459 + 0.98564736 * d) % 360
    L = math.radians((q + 1.915 * math.sin(g) + 0.020 * math.sin(2 * g)) % 360)
    e = math.radians(23.439 - 0.00000036 * d)
    declination = math.degrees(math.asin(math.sin(e) * math.sin(L)))
    right_ascension = math.degrees(math.atan2(math.cos(e) * math.sin(L), math.cos(L))) / 15
    equation_of_time = (q / 15 - right_ascension + 12) % 24 - 12
    return declination, equation_of_time * 60  # degrees, minutes

def hour_angle(latitude, declination, altitude):
    """Hours between solar noon and the moment the sun stands at `altitude` degrees."""
    lat, dec, alt = (math.radians(v) for v in (latitude, declination, altitude))
    cos_h = (math.sin(alt) - math.sin(lat) * math.sin(dec)) / (math.cos(lat) * math.cos(dec))
    if cos_h < -1 or cos_h > 1:
        return None  # the sun never reaches that altitude today
    return math.degrees(math.acos(cos_h)) / 15

    _, eqt = sun_position(jd)
    return 12 + timezone - longitude / 15 - eqt / 60  # local clock hours

    """Local clock time when the sun reaches `altitude`, before noon (-1) or after (+1)."""
    t = solar_noon(jd, longitude, timezone)  # first guess, then refine at the event itself
    for _ in range(2):
        dec, _ = sun_position(jd + (t - timezone) / 24)
        h = hour_angle(latitude, dec, altitude)
        if h is None:
            return None
        t = solar_noon(jd + (t - timezone) / 24, longitude, timezone) + direction * h
    return t

    """Sun altitude when a stick's shadow is its noon shadow plus shadow_factor times its height."""
    noon_zenith = abs(latitude - declination)  # degrees between the noon sun and straight overhead
    return math.degrees(math.atan(1 / (shadow_factor + math.tan(math.radians(noon_zenith)))))

Three things in this table deserve explanation, because a comparison that only reports agreement is not a comparison.

The **Asr** column is the one systematic difference. libmuslim evaluates Asr from the sun's declination at the start of the day and does not refine at the event, while the code in this series refines, as it does for every other prayer. In Jakarta that is 16 seconds and never changes the printed minute. In London it changes the printed minute on about two thirds of the days of the year, always by a single minute, and the refined value is the more accurate of the two. That is a finding about libmuslim rather than about this series, and it is a one-line fix there: solve Asr the way the other four prayers are solved.

The **84 minutes** in the Isha column are a single day in each city: 15 July in London and 18 August in Oslo. On those days the sun does, barely, reach 17° below the horizon, a few minutes before solar midnight. This series takes that real event. libmuslim notices that the day sits on the edge of the season and substitutes, which is what the published MWL tables do. Both are defensible readings of "the twilight has disappeared". If you are writing a timetable for a real community, follow the tables.

The **Tromsø** row is the polar case from earlier. libmuslim borrows a 45° day and fills every cell. This series leaves 265 of them blank. The 32 seconds on Maghrib is the one place they both compute something and differ, on the days when the sun just grazes the horizon and the two refinement strategies catch it at slightly different moments.

Everything else, Fajr, Dhuhr and Maghrib at every latitude and Isha on 363 days out of 365, agrees to within ten seconds, and all of it well inside the minute that any timetable prints.

## What the series showed

A prayer time is a sentence in a hadith about the sun. The sun is predictable, so the sentence can be computed. Two numbers come from the date alone, the sun's declination and the equation of time. Those plus your longitude give solar noon, which is Dhuhr. Noon plus an hour angle gives the moment the sun reaches any altitude: 0.833° below the horizon for sunrise and Maghrib, a twilight angle for Fajr and Isha, a shadow angle for Asr. That is the whole of the astronomy, and it fits in a hundred lines in any language.

Everything else is people. The twilight angle is a measurement made by the scholars of each country under their own sky, and the differences between countries are differences of sky, of reading, of precaution and of authority, every one of them traceable to the first generations. The far north is a fatwa about estimation. The clock is a decision by a government. An honest app computes the astronomy exactly, applies the authority's rules and nothing more, gets the clock from a source that knows the law, and says plainly when it is estimating.

That is what [libmuslim](/docs/libmuslim/c/Prayer%20times%20API/overview) tries to do and what Muslimtify shows you on your desktop. If you have read this far you now know every number in it, and where each one came from. Bring a stick.
